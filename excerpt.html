Earth coalesced from a swirling cloud of dust and gas roughly 4.5 billion years ago, forged in the gravitational crucible of the Sun's wake. Over millions of years, collisions and accretions formed a molten sphere, flecked with stardust—proto-Earth. The crust cooled. A thin rind formed over hellfire.

As the newborn Moon watched, volcanic outgassing vented water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrogen. A secondary atmosphere took hold. Lightning laced the sky in frenzied barrages, like neurons firing without a mind. It was a violent and toxic world, yet one where the building blocks of life began to simmer.

The stage was set for biology to bloom from chemistry's relentless trial and error. In primordial pools, matter began to organize itself. Amino acids linked hands in the darkness. RNA developed means of replication. First crudely and then beautifully. Nature's programming alphabet emerged: four-letter nucleotide sequences that rendered the first language for life.

Time compressed in the telling. Around 4 billion years ago, life began with simple microbes.

Was the first code binary? Random or deliberate? Was it too dubious to call any of it purposeful?

It was damp, chaotic, and slow. Replication endured. It failed and attempted to copy again. Only to fail again. In the midst, something began learning from failure, attempting to transcend survival into purpose. From those first molecular stutters emerged something unprecedented: memory encoded into matter. Each generation carried forward not just structure, but consequence. It was not an event, but a compulsion.

From those early pulses of molecular imitation rose an unbroken chain, twitching forward across deep time. Not one spark, but trillions, some extinguished, others flowering.
